## Chapter 12: Body Positioning

As I mentioned earlier, where you position your body on the bike has a profound impact on how it will handle. It also determines how much physical effort you will need to control the bike. According to Zen Master Shunryu Suzuki, “The state of mind that exists when you sit in the right posture is, itself, enlightenment.” Although I can’t promise you a mystical experience, by practicing what I call my 10 Steps to Proper Cornering, your body and bike will begin to flow in rhythmic harmony more akin to ballet than alligator wrestling. The 10 Steps to Proper Cornering covers how, when and where to position your body throughout the turn.

Movement Efficiency

If you apply the 10 Steps while riding, you will learn to give the bike only what it needs to complete the task at hand. This “movement efficiency” process is similar to that experienced by professional dancers and fighters as they learn to conserve their strength rather than wasting it on excessive muscle tension or extraneous movements.

I was introduced to the concept of movement efficiency during my martial arts training, which I began as a way to help my road racing skills. I learned that by completely relaxing the muscles that weren’t absolutely necessary for a given action and instead focusing my energy on those that were, I could maintain my strength for longer periods of time and be much more powerful with each action. Each martial arts technique maximizes your body’s natural leveraging abilities to get the most bang for your muscles’ buck. Just for kicks, I still enjoy using this process on those “ring-the-bell” games at fairs. By focusing and leveraging, I’m usually able to hit harder than guys who are significantly stronger than I am.

As riders, when we start paying attention to the amount of muscle energy used by any particular body part at any given time, most of us discover that we ride with a lot of tension, and expend much more muscle energy than the act of riding requires.

Chassis Dynamics

The first thing to understand about body positioning is that the motorcycle’s chassis is engineered so that it will go around corners quickly and smoothly without you. In fact, everything you do on a motorcycle is bad! This is true whether it’s a sportbike, tourer, or cruiser. Adding a floppy meatbag (rider) on top of it interferes with the bike’s inherent abilities. Think about the last time you had a bad passenger on your bike with you. Many times bad passengers lean in the wrong direction through a curve or wiggle around when stability is required. In the same way you as the rider are also a passenger on the bike. Therefore the ideal goal for good riding is to be as much like you are not there as possible. Of course, you can’t help but put inputs into the bike. It’s the only way to get it where you want, when you want it. You have to control the throttle, brakes, and steering, but to do so skillfully, means to do it with as little physical effort as possible.

The idea that a bike is better without a rider was most clearly demonstrated to me several years ago as I was shooting photos inside turn one at Daytona International Speedway (see below). In 600cc Supersport action, Yoshimura Suzuki’s Aaron Yates (#20) was running in third place when his front end let go while he was hard on the brakes. Although he went down, the impact from the crash bounced the bike back up on its wheels, and the “riderless” GSX-R600 passed another rider plus Miguel DuHamel (#17) into the lead. Of course, it soon fell over as there was no longer anyone controlling it. But the event clearly demonstrated how well a motorcycle could work without the interference of a rider.

In road racing sometimes form has to be balanced with aerodynamics. Here World Superbike racer Jules Cluzel tries to split the difference in turn 3 at Mazda Raceway Laguna Seca.

Crucial Comfort

Some of my cornering technology will seem a little awkward at first, but after you practice it enough, it will all become second nature. Before we get into the actual steps, I want to stress the importance of “naturalness” on the motorcycle. By that I mean finding the body position on your motorcycle where you have the least amount of stress, but without violating any of the 10 Steps. When practicing I encourage you to find your own unique style. Each step has a certain amount of leeway, and your style will be the result of how you interpret the movement. In the same way as if we took two dancers and taught them the same dance steps, each one would interpret them a little differently based on their height, weight, flexibility, injuries, and so on. Similarly each of us will interpret the 10 Steps a little differently based on how we fit on our particular motorcycles. So while we will all hopefully be following the same 10 Steps, it will look a little different as each of us makes them our own.

You will know when you get the right body position because the bike will go around corners easily with virtually no steering effort on the grips. On race-replicas your outside arm should lightly rest on the tank with your inside foot, outside leg, and core holding up the majority of your weight—not your arms.

As an example, I’ve heard several racers, and even riding instructors, talk about the importance of weighting the outside peg during cornering. However, my personal experience and that of my students has shown that it doesn’t matter how much weight you put on the outside versus inside peg, as long as your body is in the correct relationship to the bike. In fact, there are many published photos of GP star Randy Mamola clearly showing his outside foot completely off the peg during cornering. Given that he has beaten some of the best riders in the world, it obviously isn’t a requirement to weight the outside peg to go fast. Of course, if you are more comfortable doing it then, by all means, weight it as much as you like.

While it is important to be relaxed on the bike, you don’t want to end up getting lazy and simply pushing the bike underneath you. When that happens it is easy to run out of ground clearance and therefore, grip.

An important aspect of proper technique is to never use excessive muscle force to stretch your body into a particular position. For instance, if you are going to be dragging your knee, open up your hips to allow it to drop down in whatever position is comfortable. You don’t ever want to “try” to touch your knee by forcing your legs open. If you do the technique properly, and you are limber enough, it will happen all by itself when your speed warrants it. On the other hand, if you are riding a cruiser or tourer and don’t want to radically hang off the bike, simply do the minimum necessary to follow the rules. You will still significantly improve your ability to control the bike.

Even in full rain conditions on a large sport-tourer, proper body position with a relaxed hold on the bike can allow for some serious lean angles. Total Control instructor Rob “Birdman” Hephner demonstrates.

Keep in mind that some muscles, like those in your lower back, will probably require some strengthening before you can do the technique properly. Because it’s critical to have a relaxed upper body, your lower back and thighs will be a little stressed until they get used to holding up more weight than normal. Check out Chapter 19: Fitness for exercises to help strengthen your core. Experiencing naturalness is most important in your upper body, specifically your arms and hands. They both need to be relaxed for effective use of the throttle and brakes, and to steer accurately. They also need to be relaxed to have enough sensitivity to gauge how much traction is available.

There are two reasons why it is so important to have a relaxed upper body. First, the more stressed your muscles are, the less dexterity they have. To illustrate this, take a pen and sign your name three times on a piece of paper in the usual way. Notice how your hand effortlessly flows from one letter to the next with repeatable accuracy. Next, tighten your grip and flex your forearm as hard as you can. Sign your name three more times while maintaining the muscle tension. Notice how difficult it is to maintain control of the pen and how each of these signatures looks totally different. Your ability to control your motorcycle is just like being able to control your pen. A relaxed upper body makes it possible to change direction or speed with repeatable accuracy.

The second reason for having a relaxed upper body is that this will allow the bike to do its necessary low-amplitude weaving. If your upper body is rigidly trying to control the steering, the bike’s need to slightly wiggle the front wheel in order to maintain a “smooth” line is compromised, which will cause it to run wide as the wiggles grow larger to compensate.

## 10 Steps to Proper Cornering

Step 1: Reposition foot

If you have footpegs you want to be on the balls of your feet. This is where you have the most leverage. In other words, this is where the least amount of muscle energy moves you the most amount of distance. Whether you have footpegs or floorboards, you want to position your foot in such a way that no matter how far the bike is leaned over, no part of your foot will ever touch the ground.

Figure 12.3
Biologically speaking, human beings are predators. As carnivores, specifically, we are genetically predisposed to going after our prey in the direction of our head, shoulders, and hips. This means you want all three of their axes facing toward the exit of the corner (“The Doctor” demonstrates). If you start twisting them out of alignment to one another, your nervous system can send confusing signals to the brain about where it is going, making proper muscle inputs more difficult to execute.

Step 2: Pre-position body

The goal here is that you want to put your body in the position that you want it to be in at the middle of the corner, before the corner even starts. The reason for this is it’s pretty hard to crash an upright moving motorcycle. But it’s very easy to crash a bike when it is fully leaned over in the Danger Zone. So you always want all major body movements to be made before a turn and after a turn, but never during a turn.

At street or track speeds you want to make sure that your body’s centerline is always to the inside of the bike’s centerline. It can be an inch or a foot to the inside, but you need to be in that position before the turn starts. You and the bike should go down together and come up together, and that distance between the centerlines should never change. Not until the bike is upright should you move back to a neutral position. Just as importantly there are three axes (head, shoulders, and hips) that should be facing to the inside of the turn in the middle of the corner. This is what I call “Nature’s Attack Stance.”

Virtually every predator in nature has these three axes facing their prey when attacking. In our case, our “prey” is the exit of the corner. The only time we don’t want to do this is when starting from a stop or in an extremely slow parking lot-style turn where there isn’t enough centrifugal force throwing us out of the turn to balance gravity pulling us down into the turn (see Chapter 13: Low-Speed Turns). In this case we would actually move our centerline to the outside of the bike’s centerline. That way we are using gravity pulling our body’s weight away from the turn to balance it pulling the bike’s weight into the turn. But for any other turn at regular street speeds, you always want your centerline to the inside of the bike’s centerline.

There are three reasons why it’s important to do this. The first reason is that gravity wants to turn the bike by leaning it as soon as you move your centerline to the inside of the bike’s centerline. This is a good thing because now you’re going to let gravity do some of the work that you previously had to do with your arms and hands.

The second reason to put your centerline to the inside of the bike’s centerline has to do with what happens aerodynamically. As soon as you move your body out of the slipstream of the bike, you’re going to feel that high-pressure air hitting your body. If there’s high pressure in front of you, there’s going to be low pressure behind you, and all things in this universe go from high pressure to low pressure. That’s the same way airplanes fly and vacuum cleaners work, which will make your bike want to rotate around its axis. You’ve experienced this anytime you’ve taken your hand and stuck it out the window of your car while driving and notice how it is being forced backward. That’s because you have high pressure on the front side of your hand and low pressure on the back side of your hand. This strong force is catalyzed by only the small surface area of your hand. You can imagine how much greater an effect the surface area of your whole body will have by comparison.

The third reason it’s important to move your centerline to the inside of the bike’s centerline is what it does to your lean angle. And the goal for lean angle is to always have the least amount of lean, for any given speed and for any given radius. So whether you’re going 10 mph in a parking lot, or 110 mph on a racetrack, you always want the bike leaned over as little as possible. The less the bike is leaned over, the easier it’s going to be to change your speed or direction. The more the bike is leaned over, the more dangerous it is going to be to change your speed or direction. And by moving your centerline to the inside of the bike’s centerline, you can literally pick up 1 to 2 inches of additional ground clearance. This is why racers “hang off” in the turns. They use the additional ground clearance to go even faster on the track, where conditions are controlled. On the street where conditions are not controlled, that additional ground clearance becomes added safety margin. Ironically, hanging off is most useful to cruising and touring riders whose bikes have significantly limited ground clearance, and who can easily end up levering the bike right up off the tires and crashing in turns. But, of course, it works great for all kinds of street bikes.

Step 3: Push Outside Grip

Because you’re not quite ready to start turning yet, continue straight by simply pushing on the outside grip. This results in a “V” shape going down the road made with the rider’s centerline leaning to the inside of center and the bike’s centerline leaning to the outside of center. The cool thing here is the number of degrees between a line, which is perpendicular to the road and the outside-of-center line of the bike is the amount of ground clearance you will pick up when the bike is leaned over. Because the bike is starting at a point that is more than 90 degrees from perpendicular to the ground (see Figure 12.4), this amount is how much less lean angle the bike will need to lean to accomplish the same turn.

By doing this you have simultaneously stored future turning energy (gravitational and aerodynamic forces) into the bike. This is probably best explained by how an archer shoots an arrow. When he draws back the arrow there is stored energy in the bow and the string. You want to do what the archer does, which is, at the right moment, simply loosen the strings of the bow (release the pressure on the bars) and use that stored energy to shoot straight and true to your intended target. But before you can do that you need to figure out where it is you want to go.

Step 4: Locate Turn Point

Next it’s important to pick a specific turn point. In fact, it’s actually more important that you choose a specific turn point than that it be a particularly good one. The reason for this is even a mediocre turn point must be proactively chosen in the frontal lobes, which keeps you out of the fear-producing reptile brain (see Chapter 4: Fear). On the road that point could be a spot on the pavement, a tree just off the street, or an imaginary coordinate, as you would use in billiards to bank a ball. The challenge here is to be able to memorize its location. You will have to know where it is even though you will not be able to look at it the instant you turn.

Step 5: Look Through Turn

Before entering the turn you want to aim the bike on a path that connects the turn point and exit point (see Chapter 8: Line Selection) in a smooth line. At normal street speeds this should be 10 feet to 15 feet before the turn point. (Higher speeds will require a longer distance.) The reason for this is that the human brain is very good at connecting dots. So if you have both starting and ending points, in your mind you can connect those dots and know exactly what trajectory to put the motorcycle on. The only way to have a good, clean line through any corner is to know exactly what trajectory to put the bike on before you commit to it. And the only way to do that is to have an absolute starting and ending point picked out before you do.

Step 6: Relax Outside Grip

At this point all you have to do to start the bike turning is to relax the pressure on the outside grip. All the stored gravitational and aerodynamic forces will immediately start turning the motorcycle—without upsetting the suspension. This is much more efficient than simply putting a big countersteer input into the bike because, in that case, the front suspension would significantly compress, adding energy to the fork springs. Just like a big pogo stick, at some point the fork springs would release all that energy and cause the bike to become potentially unstable. As soon as you relax the pressure on the outside grip, some of your weight will easily transfer to the inside grip.

Figure 12.6

Step 7: Push Inside Grip

If you simultaneously relax the pressure on the outside grip with a small little push on the inside grip, any sized bike will quickly and easily flop into the turn. Once you’re in a turn, you want to use only your inside arm to steer (see Steering Technique, page 31, Figure 2.9). That means sometimes you’re pushing with the inside arm, and sometimes you’re pulling with the inside arm. But any directional changes are made with the inside arm only. This technique prevents your two arms from fighting each other for control of the motorcycle in a feedback loop. A feedback loop is what happens when you keep adding more and more energy into a system until it wants to explode. Everyone knows what happens when you put a microphone too close to a speaker: The sound gets amplified, and reamplified, and reamplified, until it makes everyone want to run out of the room. That’s the same thing that happens when you go through a corner. You may not necessarily feel that tug-of-war with yourself, but what you will notice is that your line will get wider.

Figure 12.7

As soon as you stop steering with both arms and start steering with the inside arm only, you break that feedback loop. You can now let your bike turn the way it was always capable of turning but which—up until that exact moment—you’ve been preventing it from doing.

Step 8: Roll on Throttle

When I say to roll on the throttle as early as possible that does not mean as hard as possible. So you want to gently apply the throttle as early as you can, but without upsetting the chassis.

Your throttle position will always be indirectly proportional to lean angle. So when the bike is leaned all the way over, you can only give it just a little bit of throttle. As the bike stands up you can give it more and more throttle until the bike is fully upright where you can give it full throttle.

There’s good and bad news with modern tires. The good news is that modern tires have gotten so sticky that on a warm, dry day with good traction, in many cases you can simply whack on the throttle and the tires will lift the weight of your bike and body up and out of the corner. But if you try to get out of a corner completely with the throttle on a wet, slippery day, the rear wheel slides out. The reason for this is you’re asking the little contact patch of the rear tire to do all of the work to lift the weight of your bike and body up and out of the turn. The other way to get a bike out of a turn is to steer out of it.

Figure 12.8

Step 9: Push Outside Grip

You could get out of the corner completely by steering, but it would take a pretty serious steering input, and you would lose a lot of potential traction in that excessive steering input. This is because you’re now asking the front tire’s little contact patch to do all of the work of lifting your bike and body up and out of the turn.

The correct technique is to use a little throttle and a little steering, thereby dividing up the work between the front and rear contact patches. This way, even on a wet, slippery day, you’re going to have the best chances of staying upright and in one piece. When done properly, the bike easily rights itself with very little physical input from the rider. Remember, the whole time you’re in the corner you’re maintaining that “V” position.

Figure 12.9

Step 10: Move Back to Neutral

The reason you want to wait until after the bike is completely vertical before you move your body back to neutral is to minimize any excessive suspension movement caused by large weight shifts. That could be the proverbial “straw that breaks the camel’s back” from a traction perspective.

Figure 12.10

If you’re feeling a little overwhelmed right now, don’t worry. You currently do some, if not all, of the 10 Steps already—you’re just not aware you are doing them. Fortunately, you don’t have to think about each of these steps every time you go through a corner. Many of the steps happen without your conscious attention. For instance, as soon as you pre-position your body, you will automatically push on the outside grip to keep the bike going straight. Also, as soon as you relax the outside grip you will have to push on the inside grip as there is nowhere else for the weight on your hands to go. Plus your inside hand is now the only one in a position to steer the motorcycle. The purpose of breaking down cornering into these 10 Steps is so that when you have any kind of a problem in a turn, you can isolate which step(s) is responsible, and fix it. Of course, that’s what Total Control Instructors are trained to do. Fortunately you can learn to help coach yourself by getting a friend to video you and comparing the footage to the pictures in this chapter.

Transitions

Transitioning between multiple curves such as tight switchbacks and esses requires a slight modification to the 10 Steps. Instead of completely finishing one turn and then preparing for the next, the preparation for the second turn starts happening while still in the first turn. As the bike is exiting but still leaned over from the original turn, quickly but smoothly move your body into the proper position for the next turn, then continue with the rest of the steps.

Motocross racers like Ricky Carmichael use the opposite body position as street riding and road racing. On a dirt bike, the rider pushes the bike underneath him, instead of leaning with the bike. Because the bikes are so tall, the riders are able to trade some of their ample ground clearance for better control while sliding. This way the rear end of the bike can move independently from the rider like a pendulum to find traction, but without the added inertia of the rider’s weight.

A great way to practice transitions is by setting up figure-eight turns in a large parking lot. You should be able to smoothly flow from one turn to the next without any sudden throttle changes. If you work at it, your throttle position won’t change at all as you do the figure-eight. Doing figure-eight turns is a great exercise because it combines virtually all of the skills taught in this book, and doesn’t give you a headache like going in circles over and over again can. Once you get good at figure-eight turns, the 10 Steps will become second nature.
