linelab · deterministic line-analysis engine

Six figures from Chapter 8, drawn by physics rather than by hand.

Each plate below started as a few lines of scene text. linelab composed the road, integrated a single-track motorcycle over it with RK4, graded the resulting line against sixteen Total Control riding checks, and drew what it found — from above, from the rider’s seat at the turn-in, the apex and the exit, and as a strip of what the hands were doing. Every figure ends with the one thing to practise.

Everything here came out of one command per figure. The engine is the only source of truth — the renderers never re-derive physics, they only draw what the solver already computed. 

node dist/cli/main.js figure figures/fig-08-01.scene --mode true --out .bake/fig-08-01
node dist/cli/main.js render .bake/fig-08-01/fig-08-01.json --views topdown,controls --mode true --out views-01
node dist/cli/main.js render .bake/fig-08-01/fig-08-01.json --views pov --line good     --look limit_point --roll level --s 12 --mode true --out stations-01

--look limit_point turns the rider’s head through the corner, --roll level holds the horizon flat and puts the lean on a dial, and --s puts the camera at one station — the turn-in, the apex or the exit of that line’s own recorded events. The whole chapter rebuilds with npm run bake:ch8.

Reading the drawings

Every symbol on the plates, and what puts it there.

The figures use one closed vocabulary. Nothing below is decoration: each glyph is emitted by a specific rule in the renderer, and the swatches here are drawn with that rule’s own geometry.

The lines

Colour is the verdict, never the role — a line is drawn in the colour of how it turned out, and its stroke weight says what part it plays.

ideal lineThe widest stroke. The line the solver stands behind for this road and entry speed.
mistake lineA narrower stroke, same solid ink. Never dashed — dashing is reserved for reference lines and sight rays.
verdict colourgood — passed every check it was in scope forcaution — stayed on the road, broke a shape rulefailing — left the road, or the rubric

Event markers

A marker is the glyph of an event the engine actually recorded. A marker with no underlying event cannot exist — so an unmarked line is a line where nothing of that kind happened, never a drawing that forgot.

turn pointHourglass — pinched at the waist. Where the rider committed the steering input.
apexOpen ring. The closest point of approach to the inside edge.
exitFilled dot, strictly inside the apex ring so the two never read alike.
releaseDouble chevron. Where brake or throttle came off.

How a line ends

Every trajectory ends in a glyph that says why it stopped. A failing line’s endpoint always sits at its termination — never wandering into the grass, never running off the frame uncropped.

reached the road endA plain arrowhead and nothing else. The line rode out the whole drawn window.
ran off the roadArrowhead at the crossing plus a short thick tick lying ALONG the road edge. All six mistake lines end this way.
stoppedA transverse bar replaces the arrowhead. The line came to rest before the road ended.
crashedAn ×-burst replaces the arrowhead. None of these six figures reaches one.

Everything else on the page

The road, the sight geometry and the callouts each have exactly one ink, and none of them can be confused with a trajectory.

road corridorFlat grey surface with a dashed white lane divider. The rider’s usable width is the lane, inset by the bike’s own margin.
what the rider cannot seeA darker wash over the road beyond an occluder. Blindness comes only from occluders, never from distance.
occluderA schematic solid — hedge, wall, bank, or (in figure 8.1) an oncoming vehicle, drawn where it actually blocks the view.
sight rayThe one dashed, arrowhead-free ink: from the rider’s eye to the furthest point they can see. Coloured by that line’s verdict.
callout leaderThin, solid, neutral grey — never a verdict colour, so a leader can never be mistaken for a line.

Where the road ends, and how far

The grey you can ride on is not the same as the band you are graded against, and the figures now draw both.

the graded corridorTwo fine dotted lines inside the tarmac: your own lane, inset by the room a motorcycle needs. Every verdict that says a line “ran wide” is measured against THESE, not against the painted edge — which is why a line can be on the road and still be wrong.
direction and distanceA chevron every ten true metres, pointing the way the rider is going. On the ideal line each one is numbered, so the figure carries one scale of distance and the others stay quiet.
the outcome, in a wordEach line says how it ended beside its own end: clean, caution, ran wide, ran off. The word is what makes the verdict survive a grey print — or a reader who cannot separate red from green.
where it was pointingPast a runoff, a hatched neutral ray continues at the heading the line left with, cut at the first thing it meets. The engine did not integrate it — it carries no arrowhead and no verdict colour, because it is a consequence, not a trajectory. Figure 8.1 is the one that asks for it.
scale barA round distance in metres and feet, plus the lane width. Without it every distance in the figure — how early the turn-in was, how much road the mistake ate — has no unit.

From the rider’s seat

The first-person frames are drawn from each line’s OWN recorded position, so two frames of the same corner differ by exactly what the mistake cost.

the horizon stays levelThe engine can roll the whole frame with the bike — the horizon angle IS the lean. For the book the camera is held upright instead and the lean moves to a dial, because a reader of a still picture has no sense of balance to cancel a 30° tilt with: it reads as a road falling over, not as a rider leaning.
lean dialTop right of every frame: how far the bike is leaned, in degrees, against a level ground line.
your own mirrorsBar ends and mirrors in the near corners. A first-person view with none of the bike in it gives the reader nothing to sit on.
“your line” markerWhen the rider is looking somewhere their own line does not go, the line leaves the frame — and an arrow on the edge says which way it went, rather than the frame quietly showing no line at all.

The double apex, and the cost of an early one

Three linked corners taken as one. Touch the inside of the first too soon and there is no geometry left for the second — the early-apex line is off the outside edge before the third corner exists.

Practise thisOn linked corners, give something up in the first one. Plan the pair as one shape and arrive at the second corner placed, not fast.

This ideal line is graded caution, and that grade is part of the lesson. The rubric scores every corner on its own; on a linked chain the middle corner is really just the transit between two real ones, so the line touches its inside early and takes a shape fail for it. A line that apexes each corner of a compound corner in turn is already compromised — which is exactly what the figure is about.

# Fig 8.5 — the double apex: where you first touch decides whether you make the second.
# bookDoubleApex is one linked run (c1 R12 ^70 | c2 R24 ^40 | c3 R12 ^70): two
# tight touches bridged by an opening. `good` is the chained line the engine can
# actually find — contained through all three, but graded `caution`, because the
# corner-scoped doctrine reads the linking c2 as a corner of its own and marks its
# 5%-of-sweep apex early. That grade IS the lesson: a line that apexes each corner
# in turn is already compromised on a compound corner.
# `early` turns in ten metres sooner (`premature`, the kind D25 renamed from
# `early_apex`). It touches the inside of c1 at 51% of sweep — a textbook early
# apex, hard on the kerb — and the geometry it hands itself for c2 is unrideable:
# it runs wide and off the outside edge before c3 exists.
# The two-touch solver (`style=double_apex`) refuses on this road at EVERY entry
# speed tried, 18-36 km/h — the compound-window drift arithmetic in
# solve/doubleApex.ts cannot widen back out to c2's inside edge. That refusal is a
# recorded, tripwire-tested design decision, so this figure draws the chained line
# the engine stands behind rather than a two-touch line it cannot justify.
road:      preset bookDoubleApex
lines:
  good:    ride entry=30 turnIn=auto
  early:   mistake premature
marks:     turn_point,apex
labels:
  apex#1@good           "first touch"
  apex#1@early          "early apex - hard on the inside of c1"
  run_wide_detect@early "no geometry left for c2 - off the outside edge"

Analysis

What twelve graded lines actually say.

Six ideal lines, six mistakes, one rubric of sixteen checks. Read across all of them rather than one plate at a time and five things fall out — and the first is not what riders usually blame.

01Not one of these six is a grip failure.

Every mistake line ends off the road. Every one of them gets there with more friction in reserve than the ideal line it is drawn against, and none of them out-leans it. The failure is geometric — the rider spends the corner’s width in the wrong order and runs out of road, at a lean angle and a grip margin the tyres never noticed. grip_min is the friction-ellipse margin, so a bigger number is further from the limit.

Three caveats worth stating plainly

The proportion gate is not a physics verdict. It checks whether the drawing is honest about scale, and these bakes ran in --mode true, the identity transform: literal metric proportion, no frame rotation. That makes the gate report fail or warn on the tighter figures, and it means figure 8.6's authored orient=90 is recorded in the manifest but not applied. Diagram-mode compression, which is what would satisfy the gate, is deferred past v1.0 and recorded in the project's deviations log. The geometry is correct; the gate is measuring page composition.

Figure 8.5's ideal line is the chained one, not a two-touch one. The double-apex solver refuses on this road — NO_SOLUTION / no_two_touch_line — and it refuses at every entry speed probed from 18 to 36 km/h, so this is a solver capability gap rather than a fact about 30 km/h: the compound-window drift arithmetic cannot widen the line back out to the middle corner's inside edge. Rather than draw a line the engine cannot justify, the scene asks for the chained line it can. That line is graded caution, and finding 04 above explains why that grade is the figure's lesson rather than its defect.

The rider-POV pairs are two cameras, not two roads. Both frames in a plate are the same corner at the same point of the road. What differs is where each line put its own rider, so the pose, the lean that rolls the horizon, and every number in the strip are that line's own recorded samples. Nothing in the POV is re-derived: the limit-point chevron consumes the very same field the top-down sight ray points at.
